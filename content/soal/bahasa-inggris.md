Ecology is the science of the relationships among living things and with their environment. One of the first lessons of ecology is that all forms of life in any particular environment depend on other forms of life and on their physical surroundings. In nature, there is an interdependence of living things in a particular area. Green plants take in nitrogen and other minerals from the soil and combine them with water and carbon dioxide from the air. With energy from sunlight, plants produce sugar and starches and release oxygen. Animals feed on plants or other animals. Polar bears depend on seals, and seals depend on fish, which in turn depend on tiny marine animals that feed on microscopic plants.
When a natural ecological balance is destroyed, the result can be a disaster. The example of wolves and deer in Alaska shows how the misuse of pesticides caused the *devastation* of an entire ecological system. The deer ate grass and plants that were sprayed with pesticides meant to kill insects. The chemicals passed up the food chain, and animals that ate the plants were poisoned. When the wolves ate the poisoned deer, they too were affected.

**101.** The Ocean's Living Lights

Bioluminescence is the ability of living organisms to produce their own light through a chemical reaction inside their bodies. This phenomenon occurs in a wide range of marine creatures, from tiny plankton to deep-sea anglerfish, and even some species of jellyfish and squid.

The light is produced when a molecule called luciferin reacts with oxygen, a process usually triggered by an enzyme known as luciferase. Unlike a light bulb, which wastes most of its energy as heat, bioluminescent light is remarkably efficient, releasing almost no heat at all.

Marine organisms use this glowing ability for several purposes. Some, like the anglerfish, use a glowing lure to attract prey in the pitch-black depths of the ocean. Others, such as certain species of squid, release a cloud of glowing liquid to confuse predators and escape, similar to how an octopus releases ink. Meanwhile, some plankton light up simply when disturbed by movement in the water, which is why ocean waves sometimes appear to sparkle with blue light at night, a phenomenon that has fascinated sailors for centuries.

Scientists are now studying bioluminescence for practical applications beyond biology. The genes responsible for producing this light have been used in medical research to track the activity of specific cells, and engineers have even proposed using bioluminescent bacteria as a natural, energy-efficient alternative to streetlights in the future.

Bioluminescence is the ability of living organisms to produce their own light through a chemical reaction inside their bodies. This phenomenon occurs in a wide range of marine creatures, from tiny plankton to deep-sea anglerfish, and even some species of jellyfish and squid.

The light is produced when a molecule called luciferin reacts with oxygen, a process usually triggered by an enzyme known as luciferase. Unlike a light bulb, which wastes most of its energy as heat, bioluminescent light is remarkably efficient, releasing almost no heat at all.

Marine organisms use this glowing ability for several purposes. Some, like the anglerfish, use a glowing lure to attract prey in the pitch-black depths of the ocean. Meanwhile, some plankton light up simply when disturbed by movement in the water, which is why ocean waves sometimes appear to sparkle with blue light at night, a phenomenon that has fascinated sailors for centuries.

Scientists are now studying bioluminescence for practical applications beyond biology. The genes responsible for producing this light have been used in medical research to track the activity of specific cells, and engineers have even proposed using bioluminescent bacteria as a natural, energy-efficient alternative to streetlights in the future.

**126.** How Do Noise-Cancelling Headphones Work?

Noise-cancelling headphones have become increasingly popular among travelers and office workers seeking to block out distracting background sounds. Although they might seem like ordinary headphones, they rely on a clever application of physics called "destructive interference" to actively reduce unwanted noise.

Inside each earcup, a tiny microphone continuously listens to the surrounding environment, picking up sounds such as engine hums or chatter. A built-in processor then analyzes these sound waves and instantly generates an "anti-noise" sound wave that is the exact opposite, or inverted version, of the incoming noise.

When these two waves, the original noise and the generated anti-noise, meet, they cancel each other out, since the peak of one wave aligns perfectly with the trough of the other. This process happens so quickly that the listener never hears the original background noise, only the cancellation effect combined with whatever audio, such as music or a podcast, they have chosen to play.

It is worth noting that noise-cancelling technology works best on constant, low-frequency sounds like engine drones or air conditioner hums, but is far less effective against sudden, unpredictable sounds like a dog barking or a door slamming, since the processor needs a consistent wave pattern to generate an accurate cancellation signal in time.

What physics principle do noise-cancelling headphones rely on, according to the text?

A. Reflection.
B. Destructive interference.
C. Diffraction.
D. Refraction.
E. Resonance.

**Kunci: B**
